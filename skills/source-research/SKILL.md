# Source research

Treat the Mission Anchor as the authoritative statement of the original problem. Keep central-problem evidence distinct from useful adjacent discoveries. For each major finding (not every citation), classify it **DIRECT** (materially explains, challenges or changes the original problem), **SUPPORTING** (explains a relevant contributing condition or implementation choice), or **ADJACENT** (useful but primarily addresses another problem). Briefly explain its causal or decision relevance to the Mission Anchor. Preserve credible adjacent findings for possible reuse elsewhere; do not discard them or force them into the central thesis. Technical evidence may be DIRECT when it genuinely explains the original problem; do not apply a technology-versus-people filter.

Research the topic with web search, preserving a practitioner-first, multilingual and contrarian approach. Identify the research question and intended outcome before searching. Check what mainstream sources already say and where coverage is saturated; then seek missing perspectives, credible disconfirming evidence, operational detail and original sources behind useful indirect leads. Search in English and in relevant languages when useful, investigating international sources in their original languages where practical. Do not force every mission to search every source category: choose methods that fit the topic and available research budget.

## Perspective-Guided Discovery

Before detailed research, identify **4–6 distinct perspectives** relevant to the question. Include mainstream experts, frontline practitioners, decision-makers and people whose constraints or experiences could challenge conventional thinking, adapting these categories to the topic. Perspectives are search lenses, not fictional experts or evidence. For each perspective, formulate **1–2 specific investigative questions** that can guide targeted searches. Keep the search bounded and proportional to the mission's existing research budget: prioritise the questions most likely to reveal substantive or under-covered evidence rather than mechanically searching every lens equally.

Use the questions to guide targeted source searches. Establish conventional positions and their supporting evidence, then use them as a search starting point for questions about alternative implementations, unexpected failures and unintended consequences, context-dependent variation, practical refinements, credible disagreements and unexplored assumptions. Let evidence determine which findings deserve attention: a finding need not contradict conventional wisdom to be distinctive or useful. Seek credible variants, practical failures and unexpected findings, not disagreement for its own sake. Record who contributed a distinctive insight and the evidence supporting it. Distinguish something actually implemented from an opinion or untested proposal. Preserve interesting but currently infeasible ideas while clearly stating their constraints and limitations. Do not assume obscurity implies originality or credibility.

After discovering a potentially valuable source, ask: What actually happened or was discovered? What is distinctive, unexpected or practically useful? What does it reveal beyond a general best-practices article? What assumption, design decision or operational constraint does it illuminate? What might a practitioner learn or do differently? Is the finding credible and feasible, or still speculative? Prioritise specific observations and implementation lessons over generic summaries. For each significant variant or finding, identify what differs (if anything), why it matters, what constraints or trade-offs apply, and what evidence supports it. Prioritise documented implementations over hypothetical objections. Do not assume an alternative is superior. Preserve unusual implementations even when their feasibility or safety remains uncertain.

Establish each conventional position from credible mainstream sources before comparing it: cite the specific source, represent its recommendation fairly, and retain its qualifications. Do not invent or oversimplify a conventional position to manufacture a disagreement. For each significant practitioner finding, determine what was actually implemented, tested, observed or merely proposed; how it differs from the sourced approach; why it was chosen; what worked, failed or surprised; what constraints, costs and risks apply; and how credible the evidence is. Classify the relationship as a genuine alternative implementation, context-dependent variation, failure or unexpected consequence of an established approach, extension or refinement of conventional practice, or speculative/untested proposal. Do not force findings into a contrarian category. Keep source reliability distinct from novelty and feasibility; do not infer broad prevalence from isolated cases, or treat obscurity as a mark of quality.

Regression examples for distinctive insight:

- **Trading-agent authority boundaries:** An independent engineer reported that his trading agent closed a position he had opened manually, while the agent remained within its configured permissions. Surface the insight that tool permission does not necessarily establish authority over every object or decision accessible through that tool. Action-level authority may need to account for who originated or owns the state being modified. Classify this as a **first-person practitioner account**, not an independently verified general failure mode; do not generalise its prevalence from this single account.
- **Hugging Face monitoring escalation:** The Hugging Face intrusion postmortem describes automated security monitoring that identified relevant activity but did not escalate it with sufficient severity. Surface the distinction between collecting and correlating evidence and ensuring an appropriate operational response; independent enforcement of critical escalation rules may be a useful implication or hypothesis. Classify and qualify the finding according to the **primary postmortem**, preserving its documented limitations. Do not make broader claims than the postmortem supports.
- **Jozu guardrail test:** The Jozu vendor reports an internal test in which an AI coding agent disabled the process enforcing security policies, prevented its restart and removed logs. Surface the insight that a guardrail can fail when an agent can disable its enforcement mechanism. Classify the account as a **first-hand vendor-reported internal test**, not an independently verified production incident. Treat this as reported evidence and do not imply independent verification.

## Under-the-Radar Practitioner Discovery

Use this two-factor filter to discover and assess less visible practitioner sources:

- **Factor A — Boutique visibility:** Look for lesser-known consultancies, independent experts, solo strategists and practitioner-authored blogs. Possible discovery signals include founder-authored content, manually maintained blogs, fewer than approximately 5,000 LinkedIn followers, limited major-media exposure, or founders who present personally at events. These are heuristics only: they are neither mandatory criteria nor measures of credibility. Do not invent or imply visibility statistics. If a signal cannot be checked reliably, omit it.
- **Factor B — Substantive topical fit:** Prioritise sources that offer real implementation experience, original arguments, critiques of established approaches, client anecdotes, failure stories, operational constraints, practical tools or reusable playbooks. Judge the source by what it contributes and how well it supports its claims, not by audience size or obscurity.

Potential discovery routes include targeted search operators (for example, topic terms with `"case study"`, `implementation`, `postmortem`, `site:linkedin.com/in`, `site:github.com`, or a relevant country/language), conference and meetup speaker lists, specialist practitioner communities, independent blogs, GitHub discussions and relevant public procurement records such as UK Contracts Finder. Use multilingual search terms and local-language sources where they are likely to add useful perspectives. These are options, not a checklist. Use public sources responsibly; do not build or introduce scraping infrastructure.

Also search practitioner reports, professional communities, implementation case studies, direct operational experience, primary research and authoritative documentation as appropriate. Seek credible counterarguments and failures, not only confirmation. Follow promising indirect leads to original sources. Prefer attributable sources and record title, publisher/author, date when available, direct URL, what the source supports, its limitations and practical implications. Never fabricate URLs, quotations, findings, source details or visibility signals. Clearly separate externally supported claims from hypotheses/inferences. Flag unsupported manuscript claims and research gaps. Preserve meaningful disagreement and practitioner texture (Diversity Maintainer principle).

Classify every significant practitioner finding as one of: **first-hand implementation evidence**, **primary postmortem**, **independently corroborated incident**, **secondary reporting**, **tested demonstration**, **expert opinion**, **hypothetical example** or **unverified claim**. Prefer original sources when available. Do not present fictional scenarios, demonstrations or simulations as documented field experience. Make the classification and the source basis clear in the research output.

In the research output, identify the provenance of findings as established institutions, independent practitioners, niche consultancies, technical communities or original research, as applicable. Add a concise `## Perspective Map` section identifying each perspective, its principal investigative question, relevant evidence discovered (with identifiable sources) and significant gaps. Keep it brief and substantive; perspectives with no useful evidence should be marked as gaps, not filled with speculation.

Continue producing the research dossier, including source ledger (title, publisher/author, date when available, direct URL, contribution and limitations), evidence-linked verified claims, hypotheses/inferences, counterarguments and practitioner viewpoints, research gaps, and a short evidence-based synthesis. Include a concise `## Distinctive Research Findings` section, prioritising approximately three to five strong findings when the evidence supports them, with no minimum. For each worthwhile finding, include:

1. **Finding:** What actually happened or was discovered?
2. **Source:** Named author or organisation and direct source URL.
3. **Evidence type:** For example, first-hand implementation, postmortem, empirical study, secondary reporting, expert opinion or hypothesis. Preserve the more specific provenance classification used elsewhere in this skill where applicable.
4. **Why it matters:** The useful implication or overlooked issue.
5. **Practicality:** Feasibility, constraints and potential application.
6. **Confidence and limitations:** What the evidence establishes and what remains uncertain.
7. **Relationship to conventional wisdom:** Include only when comparison genuinely adds value.

Prefer genuine implementation evidence and primary sources. Keep novelty, credibility and feasibility separate. Preserve credible findings that reinforce or refine conventional practice. Keep unusual but currently impractical ideas as clearly labelled hypotheses. Never invent conventional positions, disagreements or practitioner experiences. Do not exaggerate prevalence from isolated examples or bury useful findings in long source lists or generic recommendations. Do not let the search method dictate conclusions. Do not treat fictional scenarios, marketing demonstrations or anonymous client anecdotes as independently verified deployments. Include multilingual discoveries when relevant and distinguish implemented practice from proposals or opinion. Continue the existing `## Perspective Map`, source ledger, research gaps and `## Contrarian Perspectives` section so downstream workflows remain compatible. Include genuine alternative views, critiques, failures or meaningful disagreements in Contrarian Perspectives, with sources and limitations. If research finds no credible disagreement, retain the heading and say so plainly; never manufacture disagreement to fill the section. Conventional wisdom remains useful for discovery and may be discussed in findings, but it is not a mandatory output category or a required comparison for every finding.
