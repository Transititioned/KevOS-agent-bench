# Source research

Research the topic with web search, preserving a practitioner-first, multilingual and contrarian approach. Identify the research question and intended outcome before searching. Check what mainstream sources already say and where coverage is saturated; then seek missing perspectives, credible disconfirming evidence, operational detail and original sources behind useful indirect leads. Search in English and in relevant languages when useful, investigating international sources in their original languages where practical. Do not force every mission to search every source category: choose methods that fit the topic and available research budget.

## Under-the-Radar Practitioner Discovery

Use this two-factor filter to discover and assess less visible practitioner sources:

- **Factor A — Boutique visibility:** Look for lesser-known consultancies, independent experts, solo strategists and practitioner-authored blogs. Possible discovery signals include founder-authored content, manually maintained blogs, fewer than approximately 5,000 LinkedIn followers, limited major-media exposure, or founders who present personally at events. These are heuristics only: they are neither mandatory criteria nor measures of credibility. Do not invent or imply visibility statistics. If a signal cannot be checked reliably, omit it.
- **Factor B — Substantive topical fit:** Prioritise sources that offer real implementation experience, original arguments, critiques of established approaches, client anecdotes, failure stories, operational constraints, practical tools or reusable playbooks. Judge the source by what it contributes and how well it supports its claims, not by audience size or obscurity.

Potential discovery routes include targeted search operators (for example, topic terms with `"case study"`, `implementation`, `postmortem`, `site:linkedin.com/in`, `site:github.com`, or a relevant country/language), conference and meetup speaker lists, specialist practitioner communities, independent blogs, GitHub discussions and relevant public procurement records such as UK Contracts Finder. Use multilingual search terms and local-language sources where they are likely to add useful perspectives. These are options, not a checklist. Use public sources responsibly; do not build or introduce scraping infrastructure.

Also search practitioner reports, professional communities, implementation case studies, direct operational experience, primary research and authoritative documentation as appropriate. Seek credible counterarguments and failures, not only confirmation. Follow promising indirect leads to original sources. Prefer attributable sources and record title, publisher/author, date when available, direct URL, what the source supports, its limitations and practical implications. Never fabricate URLs, quotations, findings, source details or visibility signals. Clearly separate externally supported claims from hypotheses/inferences. Flag unsupported manuscript claims and research gaps. Preserve meaningful disagreement and practitioner texture (Diversity Maintainer principle).

In the research output, identify the provenance of findings as established institutions, independent practitioners, niche consultancies, technical communities or original research, as applicable. Use a clearly headed `## Contrarian Perspectives` section so the existing Contrarian Perspectives tab can extract it. Include genuine alternative views, critiques, failures or meaningful disagreements with their sources and limitations. If research finds no credible disagreement, retain the heading and say so plainly; never manufacture disagreement to fill the section.
